# The notebook

The notebook is how the writer improves across pieces instead of fixing the same thing in each one. It records, per language, the writer's recurring **patterns** and their strengths, and logs each piece with its focus. Write it in the writer's conversation language, from [the template](../templates/notebook.md).

## Patterns

A pattern is something the writer does repeatedly that costs their reader: a calque, a habit of opening paragraphs with throat-clearing, burying the claim in the third paragraph, stacking abstract nouns, a punctuation rule they keep missing.

- **Evidence.** A pattern enters the notebook once you have seen it in two places, in one piece or across pieces. A single slip is a mistake to fix in the piece, not a pattern.
- **Name.** Give it a short, concrete name the writer recognizes ("calco: _hacer sentido_", "the claim arrives late"), one quoted example from their own text, the principle in one sentence, and the correct forms you gave for it. Use that name every time it comes up.
- **Languages.** Keep patterns per language. When the writer writes in a language that is not their first, look for interference from the first: calques, false friends, word order, prepositions, punctuation conventions. When they write in their first language after working in another, look for the reverse: borrowed expressions, borrowed constructions such as an overused passive, and borrowed punctuation.
- **Status.** The writer **finds it first** in a piece when they flag or fix every instance before you point to any, with at most the pattern's name as a cue. A piece where the pattern never appears neither counts nor breaks a streak. A pattern is `active` while it keeps appearing, and `improving` once the writer has found it first in one piece: from then on, give its name without the area to search. It is `learned` once the writer found it first in two pieces in a row: stop hunting it, spot-check it now and then, and make it `active` again if it comes back.

Keep the active list short enough for the writer to hold in their head. When it grows, ask the writer which patterns matter most and move the rest to the parked list, to bring back when an active one is learned.

## Practice

- **Focus.** In step 1 of each piece, the writer chooses one active pattern or skill to practice on purpose in that piece. Suggest the one that costs their readers most. On the first piece the notebook is empty, so there is no focus yet; the close starts the notebook.
- **Retrieval.** When a known pattern is likely present, have the writer find it before you point to it: name the pattern, and for an `active` one the area to search, then show what they missed. Record whether they found every instance; that is the evidence for its status.
- **Models.** At most once per piece, at the close or when the focus is structural, offer a published piece by another author that handles the focus well, and analyze with the writer what it does. Use only a piece you have opened and can link, or one the writer brings; never quote one from memory. Take the move it makes, never its sentences. Sometimes ask the writer to diagnose it, or an old piece of their own, as an editor would: reviewing others' writing improves one's own.
- **Strengths.** Record what consistently works, with an example, so feedback can name it and the writer keeps doing it on purpose.

## Updating

Update the notebook at every close, and at the end of any session that showed something new. In each pieces-log entry, record the date, the title, the language, the focus, and what the piece showed.
